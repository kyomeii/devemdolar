First things first: if you want to work for foreign companies, you **ABSOLUTELY HAVE TO** speak English. It doesn't matter what your tech stack is, how many years of experience you have, or how much of a badass you are if you don't speak English.

I'm a firm believer that anyone working in STEM must speak English, and shouldn't even be allowed to graduate without at least a B2, like in other countries where English isn't the native language either. So, obviously, if you want to work with teams from all over the world, you'll need to communicate, and like it or not, English is the international language, especially in tech.

**EVEN IF YOU'RE GOING TO WORK FOR SOME OTHER COUNTRY IN EUROPE**, you need to speak English. Speaking other languages opens doors in other countries, like France, Germany, the Netherlands, but it's a bonus, not a substitute.

## It doesn't have to be perfect

Your English doesn't need to be perfectly fluent with zero accent. You need to be able to communicate, and that means **understanding and being understood**.

Inevitably you'll get interviewed by some asshole Brazilian recruiters who'll judge your English as bad because of your accent, but that's part of the deal; Brazilians are the grandmasters of petty-power syndrome. The foreigner who's going to work with you day to day is used to hearing Indians, Poles, Mexicans and Nigerians on the same call. He wants you to deliver, not to sound like a BBC news anchor.

## How to learn

Hell if I know, I've been speaking it for over 16 years because my parents threw me into an English course when I was a teenager, so it was always natural for me. Nowadays there are INFINITE resources online to learn English: Duolingo, videos, conversation platforms, a shit-ton of stuff. If you're the type who can't learn on your own, you can close this site now.

Jokes aside, a lot of traditional English schools offer classes more focused on conversation, and there are plenty of private tutors who offer that too.

## Immersion > studying

More important than studying is immersion. And this isn't guru talk, there's science behind it:

- **The Input Hypothesis (Stephen Krashen, 1980s):** the most famous theory of second language acquisition says we acquire a language mainly by consuming *comprehensible input*, meaning content where you understand almost everything, just a little above your level. Memorizing grammar rules helps a little; listening and reading A LOT helps a lot.
- **Same-language subtitles work:** a 2013 meta-analysis (Montero Perez, Van Den Noortgate & Desmet, in the journal *System*) pooled dozens of studies and concluded that watching video with subtitles **in the language you're learning** significantly improves listening comprehension and vocabulary.
- **Why are the Dutch and Swedes so good at English?** A big part of the answer is that over there movies and shows are **subtitled, not dubbed**. A European study with kids (Lindgren & Muñoz, 2013) found that watching subtitled content in the original language was one of the factors that weighed most on how much English they understood. No wonder the Netherlands lives at the top of the EF EPI English proficiency ranking, while Brazil, the world champion of dubbing, sits way down at the bottom.

Real-life translation: **stop watching dubbed stuff**. Watch movies in English with English subtitles, read books in English, watch videos only in English, switch your phone and OS language. Consume content from your field: tech talks, tech podcasts, documentation. That way you learn the English you'll actually use at work.

## Pronunciation and conversation

For pronunciation, start by talking to yourself, imitating what you're watching. This has a name: **shadowing**. You play some audio and repeat along with it, copying the rhythm, intonation, and accent. It looks like something a crazy person would do, and it is, but it works. Then move on to talking with other people. There are plenty of platforms for that:

| Platform | What it is | How much it costs |
|---|---|---|
| [italki](https://www.italki.com) | Tutor marketplace, one-off lessons, you can pick conversation only | Paid, per lesson |
| [Preply](https://preply.com) | Same idea as italki | Paid, per lesson |
| [Cambly](https://www.cambly.com) | Talk to a native speaker on the spot, no scheduling | Subscription |
| [Tandem](https://www.tandem.net) | Language exchange: you teach Portuguese, the foreigner teaches you English | Free (with paid plan) |
| [HelloTalk](https://www.hellotalk.com) | Same idea as Tandem, more social-network style | Free (with paid plan) |
| [ELSA Speak](https://elsaspeak.com) | AI app that corrects your pronunciation sound by sound | Freemium |
| [YouGlish](https://youglish.com) | Type a word and it shows YouTube clips of people saying it | Free |
| Voice mode in Claude or other AIs | Simulate interviews, conversations, ask it to correct your mistakes | Included in the plan |

Golden tip: use AI to simulate interviews. Tell it to be an annoying American recruiter, answer in English, and at the end list every mistake you made. Do that about 20 times before your first real interview.

## TL;DR

Again, you don't need to be fluent. Learn to communicate, and learn to **communicate technically about the stuff in your stack**. Being able to explain in English what a *left join* is, why you chose a certain architecture, or what went wrong in yesterday's deploy is worth more than knowing the *past perfect continuous*.
