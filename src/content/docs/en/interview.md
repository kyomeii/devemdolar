The time has come. You studied English, mastered your stack, lied responsibly on your resume, stuffed your LinkedIn with recruiters, and applied to 200 jobs. One of them got back to you. Now, this is where most people die, and it's almost never from a lack of technical knowledge, it's from a lack of **preparation**.

Interviewing is practice. The first one is going to be shit, the second a little less, and by around the fifth you'll be talking about yourself on autopilot. So: **accept every interview that comes your way, even for jobs you don't really want.** It's free practice.

## What the process looks like

Every company has its own, but the standard is more or less this:

1. **Recruiter screen (15–30 min):** a chat with someone from HR or the agency. Here they check whether your English is acceptable, whether you understand the basics of the role, your salary expectations, schedule availability, and whether you're OK being a contractor. It's not technical, but it's where a lot of people get cut for not knowing how to sell themselves.
2. **Technical round:** could be live coding, live SQL, a business case, or a take-home (a project to do at home). Depends on the stack and the level.
3. **System design:** for mid/senior roles and up. You draw an architecture and defend your choices.
4. **Hiring manager / behavioral:** a chat with whoever's going to be your boss. Questions like "tell me about a time you disagreed with someone."
5. **Final / culture fit:** at startups, it's usually with the founder or CTO. Here they want to know if you're a decent person and won't just vanish.

If it's through a **nearshore or consulting firm**, get ready to do half the process with them and then repeat the whole thing with the end client. Comes with the territory.

## Before the interview

- **Research the company:** what it sells, who its customers are, how much funding it's raised (Crunchbase), what people say about it on Glassdoor. Stalk the LinkedIn of whoever's interviewing you.
- **Reread the job post and YOUR resume.** Especially the parts you made up. If you wrote that you optimized a pipeline by 90%, be ready to explain *how*.
- **Double-check the time zone.** Invites come in EST, PST, CET, and everyone gets screwed by daylight saving time: the US changes clocks in March and November, Europe at the end of March and the end of October, and Brazil doesn't change anymore. When in doubt, put it in Google Calendar and let it convert.
- **Setup:** stable internet (have your phone's 4G as plan B), headset with a decent mic, camera at eye level, light on your face and not behind you, tidy or blurred background. Test the link 10 minutes before.
- **Camera always on.** Foreigners find it weird when someone interviews with the camera off.

## "Tell me about yourself"

It'll come up in **100% of interviews**, usually as the first question. Prepare a 1-to-2-minute answer and practice until it sounds natural. A structure that works: **present → past → future**.

> "I'm an Analytics Engineer with 5 years of experience, currently working for a US startup where I own the data stack, from ingestion to the dashboards the leadership team uses every day. Before that I spent a few years in consulting, building BI solutions for companies in retail and finance. What I enjoy most is turning messy data into something people actually use to make decisions, and that's why this role caught my attention."

Don't tell your life story since high school. Nobody cares.

## Behavioral questions (STAR)

Foreigners LOVE behavioral questions: "Tell me about a time when...". The way to answer is the **STAR** method:

- **S**ituation — the context, quickly.
- **T**ask — what your role or problem was.
- **A**ction — what **YOU** did (not "we", **I**).
- **R**esult — the result, ideally with a number.

Prepare **5 or 6 stories** you can adapt to almost any question:

1. A project you're proud of.
2. A time you screwed up big and what you learned.
3. A conflict or disagreement with a coworker or stakeholder.
4. An impossible deadline you delivered on (or didn't).
5. Something you had to learn really fast.
6. A time you went beyond what you were asked.

Write them down in English, as bullet points, and practice saying them out loud. You can use AI to play the interviewer and give you feedback.

## Technical round

It depends a lot on your stack, but there are some rules that apply to everyone:

- **Think out loud.** In live coding, the interviewer wants to see *how* you think, not just whether you get it right. 5 minutes of silence is worse than a wrong answer that's well explained. And think out loud **in English**, which takes practice.
- **Ask before you start coding.** Clarify requirements, edge cases, data size. Seniors ask, juniors start typing.
- **Practice on the right platform:** LeetCode and HackerRank for algorithms, [StrataScratch](https://www.stratascratch.com) and [DataLemur](https://datalemur.com) for SQL and data, and Alex Xu's book (it's on the [Technical page](/en/technical/#books)) for system design.
- **Take-home:** do it well, with a README explaining your decisions and what you'd do with more time. If the take-home asks for more than 6–8 hours of free work, think twice about whether it's worth it.

### What about AI in the interview?

A lot of companies today **allow** AI on the take-home, and some even in live coding. Ask first, no shame.

Now, using some hidden tool to cheat in a live interview is dumb as hell. An experienced interviewer notices your eyes darting to the corner of the screen, the weird pause before every answer, and then all it takes is one follow-up question you can't answer. And even if you pass, you'll get fired in two weeks, when they realize you don't know what you said you knew.

Lying responsibly means knowing you'll be able to learn it in time. Cheating in the interview is lying irresponsibly.

## Salary

The recruiter will ask for your expectations right on the first call. Be ready:

- **Always in dollars (or euros).** Never, ever, talk in reais. The moment you say "15k reais," you've become a 3k-dollar guy.
- **Know how to convert:** the hour is the standard contractor unit. A full month has about 160–170 hours, so US$ 30/hour ≈ US$ 5k/month ≈ US$ 60k/year.
- **Do your research:** [levels.fyi](https://www.levels.fyi), Glassdoor, and ask people who already work for foreign companies (that's what this site is for). Nearshores and agencies pay less than being hired directly by an American company. Comes with the territory.
- **Try to make them say a number first:** "What's the budget for this role?" If that doesn't work, give a range where the **minimum** is already the number you want.
- **Remember contractors don't get benefits:** no 13th salary, no paid vacation, no FGTS (Brazil's mandatory severance fund), and you pay your own taxes. The rate has to cover all of that.

When the offer comes in, yes, you can negotiate, politely, once: "I'm really excited about this role. Is there any flexibility on the rate? I was expecting something closer to X." The worst that can happen is they say no.

## Questions to ask at the end

"Do you have any questions for us?" **Never** answer "no". Have 2 or 3 ready:

- "What does success look like in the first 90 days?"
- "How is the team distributed, and how much time zone overlap do you expect?"
- "What's the biggest challenge the team is facing right now?"
- "What are the next steps in the process?"

## Scams

There are a lot of scams targeting people looking for remote jobs. Stay sharp:

- They ask for money for anything (course, equipment, "fee"). Scam.
- Interview only by chat on Telegram/WhatsApp, no video. Scam.
- Offer with no interview at all. Scam.
- They send you a check to "buy equipment" and ask you to send back the difference. Classic scam.
- They ask for your bank details, CPF (Brazilian taxpayer ID), or a photo of your ID before there's a contract. Be suspicious.
- **"Clone this repo and run the project so we can do the test":** there are hacker groups (including state-sponsored ones, like North Korea's) that create fake dev jobs just to get you to run malicious code that steals your passwords and crypto wallets. Read the code first, and run take-homes from unknown companies in a virtual machine, container, or Codespace, never directly on your computer.

## After the interview

- A short thank-you email the same day isn't mandatory, but foreigners like it.
- If a week goes by with no answer, send a polite follow-up.
- **There will be ghosting.** Lots of it. It's not personal, it's the market.
- Got rejected? Ask for feedback (it rarely comes, but sometimes it's gold), write down what you got wrong, and move on to the next one. Remember: you already had the no.

## Signed the offer? Read the contract

Before signing, read the whole contract, paying attention to:

- **Amount, currency, and payment frequency** (monthly, biweekly, hourly with timesheet).
- **Who pays the transfer fees.**
- **Notice period:** how many days on each side.
- **Exclusivity and non-compete:** if you plan on having more than one contract, this is the most important part.
- **Intellectual property:** it's normal for what you build for them to be theirs. It's not normal for what you build on the weekend in your personal project to be theirs.
- **Vacation and holidays**, which contractors usually don't get and need to negotiate.

Signed? Congrats. Now comes the part where you don't fuck it up.
